This half of the chapter was interesting because it takes the concepts and ideas of the four constraints- logical, physical, semantic, and cultural- deeper by exploring constraints that force the desired behavior. As stated by Norman, these forcing constraints are forcing functions, interlocks, lock-ins, and lockouts. Forcing functions, according to Norman, are a form of physical constraint, for they are situations in which the actions are constrained so that failure at one stage prevents the next step from happening. On pages 141 to 142, he gives an example of one, which is starting a car. Starting a car has a forcing function associated with it, as the driver must have some item that allows them and gives them permission to use the car, like keys. It is a bit complicated nowadays by the fact that many cars have many other means of permitting them to use the car, such as still requiring a key, but it can stay in one’s pocket or carrying case, and in other cases the key is not required and is replaced by a card, phone, or some other thing that can communicate with the car, but the principle still stands - as long as only verified people have the thing, it all works fine because the car won't start unless they possess the thing that makes the car turn on. After that, Norman talks about interlocks, which force operations to take place in proper sequence; lock-ins, which keep an operation active, preventing someone from prematurely stopping it; and then lockouts, which prevent someone from entering a dangerous space, or prevent an event from occurring. After discussing these, Norman then talks about conventions, constraints, and affordances, explaining that conventions are cultural constraints. People learn how familiar objects and controls usually work, and then they carry those expectations to new situations. On pages 146 to 149, Norman gives an example of this through an elevator. When elevators were first installed in the late 1800s, they had an operator who controlled the speed and direction of the elevator, stopped at the correct floors, and opened and shut the doors, and people using the elevator would enter it, greet the operator, and say which floor they wanted to go. When elevators became automated, a similar convention was followed: people entered the elevator and told the elevator what floor they were traveling to by pushing the button for their floor number. And then destination-control elevators, which have keypads located in the hallway outside the elevators and have no keypads inside the elevators, group passengers so that those going to the same floor are asked to use the same elevator. Yet despite the destination-control elevator being more efficient, it still confuses people when it changes a convention people already know, as instead of choosing a floor inside the elevator, passengers enter the destination in the hallway and are directed to an elevator.
