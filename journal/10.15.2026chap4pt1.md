This chapter was compelling to read because it goes into how designers use constraints, forcing functions, conventions, and signifiers to help guide users through unfamiliar and unknown items and situations and to prevent errors. According to Norman, there are four kinds of constraints which are physical, cultural, semantic, and logical. He introduces us to these constraints through the example of building a LEGO motorcycle, which is on pages 123 to 130. There are fifteen pieces in the set, some of which are specialized; however, the fifteen pieces themselves don't really need detailed instructions on which part fits and where each piece goes because the four kinds of constraints determine that. Physical constraints show where each piece can fit together; cultural constraints show the understanding of colors and objects; semantic constraints show the analysis and meaning of the situation, such as knowing that the rider faces forward; and logical constraints come into play when there is only one piece left remaining in the set. After discussing the four constraints, Norman then goes back into doors to explain how to apply affordances, signifiers, mappings, and constraints to everyday objects, referring back to Chapter 1 with the door example with his friend and Chapter 2 with the fire door. He then, on pages 132 to 135, discusses the large variety of doors and how they are designed, especially doors with flat plates and other handles - a flat plate implies pushing, while a regular door handle implies pulling. Norman believes that a door shouldn't require any explanation to figure out how to open it; the design itself should show and communicate the action required. Without that, a person trying to figure out how to open an unfamiliar door would try a myriad of ways to open it, even though the action and design are simple, all because there are no signifiers.
